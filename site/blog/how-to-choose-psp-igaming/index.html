How to Choose a PSP for iGaming: Order and Questions Before Signing

A wrong PSP choice costs more than it seems during negotiations: lost deposits, frozen reserves, fines without evidence and months of re-integration. Below is the order in which to source providers and the questions to settle before signing.

Contents

  2. Who you’re dealing with: acquirer, aggregator or agent

Where the choice starts

Choosing a PSP is step five, not step one. If you start with a provider “a friend already uses”, the cashier ends up with methods hardly anyone in your GEO pays with — and one provider for everything.

  1. GEO analysis — priority markets, licence restrictions, popular methods.
  2. Competitor analysis — which methods and providers similar projects use.
  3. Method list — prioritised for each GEO.
  4. 3–5 PSPs — at least two providers per key method.
  5. Onboarding — in parallel: KYB, contracts, tests.
  6. Launch — gradual, with approval rate measurement.

Two providers per method isn’t over-insurance. A single PSP means any outage, channel change or freeze takes payment acceptance to zero, and in negotiations you have no “we’ll move volume” argument.

Who you’re dealing with: acquirer, aggregator or agent

The market sells three different businesses under the word “PSP”, and that determines price, stability and who actually holds your money.

TypeWhat it isWhat to consider
AcquirerA bank or EMI licensed to hold client fundsLowest price and the strictest licence and KYB requirements
Aggregator / facilitatorConnects merchants to one or several acquirersMore flexible on requirements but more expensive. Ask which acquirers process the cards
Agent / technical gatewayAn intermediary that doesn’t hold funds and earns a commissionOne more markup in the chain. Settlement is still done by the acquirer or aggregator

Every intermediary adds its own margin and its own point of failure. If two of your PSPs sit on the same acquirer, you have no redundancy: if the acquirer has a problem, both go down.

Reputation check before the call

An hour of checking saves months of holds. Stories about delayed settlements and fines “out of thin air” usually surface straight away if you know where to ask.

  • Specialist Telegram communities on iGaming and high-risk payments. A direct “who has worked with X” usually gets answers within an hour.
  • Payment managers at other projects. In private they share more honestly than in public.
  • Registers and open sources: Companies House, OpenCorporates, courts. Company age, director changes, links via UBOs and addresses, lawsuits.
  • Private lists of unreliable providers in large chats.

Money: what makes up the price

The rate in the offer is just one line. The real cost is made up of several fees, reserves and the conversion rate.

ItemHow it’s chargedWhat to watch
MDR% of a successful transactionSeparately for FTDs and repeat deposits
Fix success feeFixed fee per successful transactionOn small tickets it can cost more than the MDR
Fix decline feeFixed fee per declineWith a low approval rate it becomes the biggest item
Settlement fee% when paying out to the merchantPlus the conversion spread
Refund / Chargeback feePer refund and per chargebackCharged regardless of the dispute outcome
Setup / Monthly feeOne-off and monthlyUsually removed in negotiation
Min. monthly / commitmentTop-up to a minimum volumeProtects the provider, not you

Compare providers by the cost of one successful deposit including approval rate, not by MDR. A cheap PSP with a low approval rate almost always costs more.

Also clarify:

  • Rolling reserve: percentage and holding period. It’s your working capital frozen for months.
  • Security deposit: a fixed amount, more often requested from new merchants and in risky GEOs. When and how it’s returned after termination.
  • Settlement cycle: T+1 and T+7 are normal; T+14 and T+30 are a reason for caution. A one-day settlement delay is the first sign of trouble at a provider.
  • Conversion rate: which source (xe.com, an exchange, their own) and what spread. A benchmark is no more than 1%.

Setup and monthly fees can almost always be removed: there are enough providers on the market without them. If a PSP insists, offer to fold them into a small MDR markup — then you only pay for real transactions.

Contract and Annex

Rates and tariffs usually live in an Annex signed separately from the main contract. That’s where unilateral changes are most often made by email, so check the Annex before every commercial review.

What must be written down:

  • At least 30 days’ notice of tariff changes. Without this clause the rate can be raised retroactively.
  • Fines: an annual cap, escalation steps (warning, then fixed amounts), a closed list of grounds.
  • Evidence for fines. For card scheme fines — the Case ID (scheme reference), a screenshot from the scheme’s system, the provider’s correspondence with the acquirer, a breakdown of the amount. For traffic violations — transaction data: ID, BIN, IP, time, amount.
  • A dispute window, for example 14 days, and deductions from the reserve only after a lost dispute.
  • Freeze and termination terms: chargeback thresholds, notice period, what happens to the reserve.

Don’t agree to fines for traffic quality (approval rate, decline rate), to multiplying a fine by each transaction, or to scheme fines re-invoiced with the provider’s markup. The main sign of a genuine scheme fine is a Case ID you can verify.

Technical questions

  1. Which decline codes are passed on. You need the original response: the issuer code and reason text exactly as returned by the bank or acquirer, not replaced by the provider’s mapping. The provider’s own classification is useful as an extra field, but if all you get instead of the original code is a generic “declined”, you can’t tune cascading or investigate a drop. Also check that the text isn’t truncated on its way through your payment gateway.
  2. How statuses and callbacks work. What happens on a timeout, are notifications resent, can a payment get stuck in pending forever.
  3. Whether FTDs and repeat deposits are split across different MIDs, and what the provider’s trusted-player criteria are: after how many successful deposits, for what amount and over what period a player counts as trusted, and whether there’s a required FTD-to-repeat ratio. These criteria must be mirrored exactly in your gateway’s routing rules. If a first deposit goes to the trusted-player MID, that breaches the contract and is grounds for a fine.
  4. Whether there’s cascading after 3DS.
  5. Whether there’s tokenisation and card storage. A repeat deposit without re-entering card details noticeably lifts conversion.
  6. How approval rate problems are escalated: a dedicated manager, response time, willingness to investigate specific transactions and switch channel or BIN.

Testing and launch

A provider’s sandbox isn’t production: methods often behave differently there. Before launch, test on production with minimal amounts and real cards: a successful payment, a decline with a clear message for the player, an abandoned payment with a correct final status, matching statuses in your system and the provider’s back office, limits and GEOs per the contract, the descriptor.

Launch a new PSP on part of the traffic alongside your current provider, on the same segments (GEO, method, FTD or repeat). That’s the only way to see the real difference in approval rate rather than the one promised in the deck.

In short

How many PSPs do you need at launch?

At least two providers per key method in each priority GEO, ideally on different acquirers. A mature project usually runs 3–4 providers with volume split between them.

What matters more — rate or approval rate?

Approval rate. A difference of a few percentage points in approval rate almost always outweighs a difference in MDR, so compare the cost of a successful deposit.
