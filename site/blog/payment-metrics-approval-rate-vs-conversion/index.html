Approval Rate, Conversion or Cost per Deposit: What to Measure

Almost every team has one number it judges payments by — usually approval rate. The problem is that one metric answers only one question. Here is how approval rate, player conversion and cost per successful deposit differ, and why relying on just one of them often hides real losses.

Three metrics, three different questions

The payments team, product and finance often all say “conversion” and mean different things. Until the definitions match, arguing about whether things got better or worse goes nowhere.

MetricQuestion it answersWhat it misses
Approval rateWhat share of submitted payments the provider and issuer approvedPlayers who left the cashier before submitting, and the cost of each attempt
Player deposit conversionWhat share of players who started a deposit actually paidWhy exactly it failed and what success cost
Cost per successful depositWhat you really pay for each credited depositHow many players you lost on the way

Approval rate depends on the denominator

The same cashier can show very different approval rates depending on what counts as an attempt. If every retry and every cascade step is a separate transaction, the rate drops. If you count only the final outcome of the player’s intent, it rises. Both numbers are “correct”, but you can’t compare them with each other, across providers or across months with different cascade logic.

There is the opposite trap too. A high approval rate is easy to get when some players never reach the submit step: the method they need is missing, the form breaks on mobile, 3DS drops out. Those losses never show up in approval rate at all.

Player conversion: closest to revenue

Conversion from pressing “Deposit” to money being credited shows what the business cares about: how many players managed to pay. It includes everything — method choice, form fields, 3DS, provider responses, clear error messages. But it is an outcome number: it tells you things got worse, not where they broke. For that you need a breakdown by step, GEO, method, provider, BIN and new vs returning players.

How you define success matters as well. If a player is declined for insufficient funds, the right response is a clear message and the player’s own choice, not attempts to push the payment through. A metric that rewards aggressive retries works against the player and against you.

Cost per successful deposit: where the money hides

The rate in the provider contract is only part of the price. The real cost of a credited deposit includes fees on successful and sometimes failed attempts, fixed fees, currency conversion, chargebacks and their handling, reserves and settlement delays. A provider with a low rate and weak approval in your GEO can cost more than a pricier but stable one.

Retries cost money too. Card schemes run programmes under which excessive reattempts on already declined transactions can incur extra fees. A cascade that lifts approval rate through extra attempts can make both cost and your relationship with the acquirer worse.

Signs you are watching the wrong number

  • Approval rate goes up, but deposits don’t. Most likely the denominator changed, or losses moved to before the payment is submitted.
  • Payments and product bring different numbers for the same period, and both are right — by their own definitions.
  • Providers are chosen on rate, without counting the full cost of a successful deposit by GEO and method.
  • The average looks fine, while approval in one GEO or with one provider dropped a while ago.
  • Reports show a generic “declined” status without the original decline codes — so there is nothing to manage approval with.

The metrics only work together

None of the three numbers replaces the others. Approval rate shows the health of routes and providers, conversion shows how many players ended up paying, cost per deposit shows what it cost. Connecting them takes a single “deposit intent” event, shared definitions across teams and data that joins the cashier, providers and finance. This is usually where it turns out some numbers were being counted differently all along.

In short

What is a normal approval rate?

There is no single benchmark: it depends heavily on GEO, methods, BINs, the share of new players and how attempts are counted. The meaningful comparison is your own traffic against itself, using the same method.

Which matters more — approval rate or conversion?

For the business, player deposit conversion and cost per successful deposit are closer to what counts. Approval rate tells you where exactly the losses happen.
