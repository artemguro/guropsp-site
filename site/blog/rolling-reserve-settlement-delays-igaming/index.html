Rolling Reserves and Settlement Delays: What They Cost an Operator

When choosing a PSP, teams usually compare the fee and the approval rate. The reserve terms and payout schedule get read last — yet they decide how much of your money is not with you at any given moment. Here is what rolling reserves and settlement delays are, why providers apply them and where operators lose money on them.

What the provider actually holds

When a player deposits by card, the money first lands with the acquirer or PSP and only then moves to the operator. Between those two points there are two mechanisms that stand between you and your own revenue.

MechanismHow it worksWhat it means for cash
Settlement delayRevenue is paid out on a schedule, a set number of business days laterSeveral days of turnover are permanently “in transit”
Rolling reserveA percentage of each payment is held and released after an agreed periodA layer of frozen cash that grows with turnover
Fixed or upfront reserveA lump sum or deposit kept with the providerMoney out of circulation from day one
Capped reserveHolds continue until the reserve reaches an agreed amountThe burden is limited, but the cap often gets revised

In a real contract these mechanisms are combined, and their parameters depend on the provider, the licence, the markets and the processing history.

Why providers do it

For an acquirer, gambling is a high-risk category. It answers to the card schemes for chargebacks and fraud on its merchants, and if an operator can’t cover disputes or shuts down, the losses fall on the acquirer. The reserve is its insurance against that. The pressure has grown: in 2025 Visa replaced its earlier dispute and fraud monitoring programmes with a single programme, VAMP, which also assesses acquirers, so providers watch risky portfolios more closely.

So a reserve is not a whim, and not always a sign of a bad provider. The question is whether it is proportionate to your actual risk and whether the contract sets clear rules for releasing it.

What it costs the operator

Held money isn’t lost, but you can’t use it. Player withdrawals, marketing, salaries and provider fees all have to be paid now. The faster turnover grows, the more money sits in reserves and in transit — growth by itself increases the need for working capital.

  • Cost of capital. Frozen amounts have to be replaced with your own or borrowed money.
  • False savings on fees. A provider with a lower rate can cost more if it holds a bigger reserve for longer and pays out less often.
  • Fragmentation. With several PSPs, money sits in different reserves, currencies and schedules, and the overall liquidity picture gets lost.
  • Reconciliation. If nobody tracks reserve release dates, holds and deductions, provider errors go unnoticed.
  • Changing terms. After a rise in chargebacks the reserve can be raised or payouts delayed exactly when the money is needed most.

What the terms depend on

Reserve terms are negotiable, not a given. They depend on the licence and jurisdiction, markets and methods, processing history and volumes, chargeback and fraud ratios, the quality of KYC and how transparent the business is. An operator with a clean history and clear reporting can push for a review over time — but only if it tracks these numbers itself and can show them.

The opposite route — splitting traffic across merchant accounts or hiding figures to stay out of monitoring programmes — breaks card scheme rules and usually ends with accounts being closed. The way to a lower reserve is real risk reduction and transparency, not workarounds.

Signs your reserves aren’t under control

  • Finance can’t quickly say how much money each provider is holding right now and when it comes back.
  • Providers were compared on rate alone, without the reserve and payout schedule.
  • Reserve releases aren’t reconciled against the contract and statements.
  • The contract has no clear terms for reviewing the reserve or releasing it on termination.
  • Cash gaps appear as turnover grows, even though the business is profitable.

What the job actually involves

Managing reserves means connecting each provider’s contract terms, the actual settlement statements, chargeback and refund data and a turnover forecast. Only then can you see what each provider really costs once frozen cash is counted, where terms are outdated and what arguments you have for negotiation. In short

Can a gambling operator take cards with no reserve at all?

Sometimes, usually with a strong processing history, large volumes and a low dispute ratio. For a new operator or a new market, some form of reserve is common.

Is the reserve returned when you leave a provider?

As a rule yes, but after the period needed to close out possible chargebacks and under the contract’s terms. That is why the release terms are worth reading before signing.
