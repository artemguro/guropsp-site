Payment Orchestrator vs In-House Gateway: What Really Differs

“Orchestrator or our own gateway?” is usually discussed like choosing a vendor. In reality it decides who controls your deposits, your data and your payment costs for years. Here is what really differs and where operators most often go wrong.

  4. What you only see after launch

What we are comparing

An orchestrator and an in-house gateway solve the same problem: connecting several providers to the cashier and controlling where each deposit goes. An orchestrator is an external platform: one integration gives you its PSP connectors, routing, cascading and reporting. An in-house gateway is your own layer between the cashier and the providers: you build and maintain the integrations, the logic and the data yourself.

From the outside both look the same — the player sees a Deposit button. The difference shows later: in how fast you can change things, in control over data and in what each successful payment costs.

The core trade-off: speed vs control

An orchestrator lets you launch quickly without your own payments team. But you work within its limits: which providers are connected, how flexible the routing rules are, what decline data you can see. Your own gateway gives full control over logic and data, but it needs people, time and ongoing maintenance — provider APIs change, and every integration failure becomes your problem.

What it really costs

An orchestrator is usually priced per transaction, so its cost grows with volume, on top of the PSPs’ own fees. With an in-house gateway the main cost is the team, integration maintenance, monitoring and the security requirements for handling card data. Don’t compare a licence fee with a developer’s salary — compare the full cost of a successful deposit at your volume, now and a year from now.

What you only see after launch

  • Card data and tokens. Where they are stored and whether you can take them with you when you leave. Without that, switching platforms can mean players have to re-enter their cards.
  • Provider coverage. Whether there are connectors to your PSPs and methods in your GEOs, and how long adding a new one takes.
  • Routing depth. “Country → provider” rules exist almost everywhere. BIN routing, sticky routing and cascades that respect decline codes and provider requirements are far less common.
  • Decline data. Whether you see the provider’s original decline codes or only a generic status. This decides whether you can manage approval rates at all.
  • A new point of failure. If the orchestrator is down, the whole cashier stops — even when every provider is working.

It is not a once-and-for-all choice

Operators often start on an orchestrator and, as volumes grow, move part of the logic or key GEOs to their own gateway — or run both. The right setup depends on volume, the number of providers, your markets and whether you have people to run it. Getting it wrong is expensive: migrating payment infrastructure takes months and puts deposits at risk.

In short

What should a new project choose — an orchestrator or its own gateway?

Most new projects launch faster and cheaper on an external platform. But check the terms on data, tokens and exiting the contract before signing — you can’t change them later.

Will an orchestrator raise approval rates by itself?

No. It gives you tools: routing, cascades, reports. Approval rates go up when those tools are tuned on your own traffic data.
