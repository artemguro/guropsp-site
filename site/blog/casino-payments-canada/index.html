Casino Payments in Canada: Cards, Interac and the Risks

Canada is often treated as one market with one currency and card-paying players. In reality the rules are set by the provinces, the key local method works differently from cards, and payment partners look more and more closely at exactly where an operator is authorised. Here is what matters for a cashier serving Canadian traffic and why it is easy to lose both deposits and payment accounts there.

Not one country but several regimes

Gambling in Canada is regulated at provincial level. So the question “can we accept Canadian players” has no single answer — it depends on the player’s province, and payment providers ask it exactly that way.

ProvinceModelWhat matters for payments
OntarioOpen market since April 2022: regulated by the AGCO, run by iGaming OntarioOperating only with registration and an agreement; the regulator’s standards cover verification, player funds and withdrawals
AlbertaOpen market since 13 July 2026: AGLC registration plus an agreement with the Alberta iGaming CorporationThe grey-market transition period ended on 13 October 2026; the government names payment processors as one lever against illegal operators
Most other provincesThe legal online offering is run by provincial lottery corporationsA private operator needs a sober view of the basis on which it accepts these players and what its PSP knows about it

For the cashier, this means “CA” is too coarse a segment. Method availability, partner requirements and even which providers will take the traffic at all can differ by province.

Which methods players actually use

  • Interac e-Transfer. The main local method: a transfer from the player’s own bank account. It is connected through payment providers that work with Interac for business, and it behaves differently from a card: a different player journey, different statuses, different reasons for unfinished payments.
  • Credit cards. The regulated markets in Ontario and Alberta allow them. But many issuers classify gambling transactions on a credit card as a cash advance, with a separate fee and interest from day one. The player often finds out from the statement, and the operator from complaints and disputes.
  • E-wallets, eCash and local methods. These usually complement Interac and cards and cover the cases where the first two fail.

Interac Online, the older pay-through-online-banking method, is no longer available, so it may still appear in cashier descriptions or provider decks only as an outdated name. In Canada the route to a deposit for many players is a bank transfer rather than typing in a card, and a cashier built “the European way” loses conversion here.

Where operators lose money and accounts

  • Licence and traffic mismatch. The provider onboarded you for one province, but traffic comes from another. With Alberta’s transition period over, such gaps become more visible, and the consequences land on your payment relationships.
  • Hidden card costs for players. Cash advance fees don’t show in your cashier, but they affect repeat deposits, complaints and chargebacks.
  • One provider for Interac. If it degrades, the most popular method has no fallback — and this is rarely visible on an overall dashboard.
  • AML workload. FINTRAC’s definition of a reporting casino includes entities that conduct and manage internet lottery schemes; one example is the casino disbursement report from CAD 10,000, including amounts to one person totalled over 24 hours. How these duties are split in your setup is a question for legal counsel.
  • Slow withdrawals. Canadian regulators expect players to get their money within a reasonable time and after proper verification. Verification dragged out at the withdrawal stage hurts both reputation and compliance.

Workarounds — disguising gambling as another business, changing the MCC or descriptor, accepting players from provinces without a basis — break card scheme rules and local requirements, and usually end with closed accounts and held funds.

Signs your cashier isn’t set up for Canada

  • Conversion is measured for the country as a whole, with no split by province and method.
  • For Interac you can’t see where players drop off — in the cashier, at the bank or at the provider.
  • Complaints about “extra” charges on credit cards are growing.
  • Nobody can quickly say which province each payment account was onboarded for.
  • Withdrawals stall on verification that could have been done earlier.

What the job actually involves

Making a cashier work for Canadian traffic means connecting provincial requirements, provider contracts, how Interac and cards behave in your own data, the player’s journey through the cashier and AML duties. A mistake in any link means either lost deposits or risk to your payment accounts. In short

Can operators accept credit card deposits in Canada?

In the regulated Ontario and Alberta markets, yes. But many issuers treat these transactions as cash advances, so players should get a clear note about possible fees from their bank, and the cashier should offer alternatives such as Interac.

Is one provider enough for Interac?

Perhaps to start with, but it is the most important local method, and without monitoring and a fallback any provider degradation hits deposits immediately.
